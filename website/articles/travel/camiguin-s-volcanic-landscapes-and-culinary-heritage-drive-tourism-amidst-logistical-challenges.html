Camiguin’s Volcanic Landscapes and Culinary Heritage Drive Tourism Amidst Logistical Challenges

By Palanyag Reporter • November 14, 2026

RAPPLER

Camiguin, the volcanic province widely recognized as the “island born of fire,” is experiencing a sustained influx of travelers seeking a rare convergence of marine biodiversity, historical landmarks, and unique culinary experiences. Despite the logistical complexities inherent in reaching this remote destination, the island’s allure remains undiminished, drawing visitors eager to explore its rugged terrain.

The province’s natural splendor is anchored by its dramatic volcanic peaks, most notably Mt. Hibok-Hibok and Mt. Vulcan. These geological sentinels serve as majestic backdrops to the island's diverse coastal features. White Island, a prominent C-shaped sandbar, remains a cornerstone of the Camiguin experience, offering visitors access to vibrant turquoise waters. However, the island's ethereal beauty is often subject to the whims of nature, as shifting cloud cover frequently veils the volcanic peaks, adding a layer of mystery to the landscape. For those pursuing a more immersive marine encounter, Mantigue Island offers a sanctuary of expansive beaches and rich underwater biodiversity. Snorkeling in its clear, shallow waters provides close encounters with diverse fish species and sea turtles, while the island’s forest boardwalk offers a shaded retreat where local birdlife thrives amidst the coastal greenery.

The island's unique geography also fosters a variety of freshwater and historical sites that tell the story of its turbulent past. The refreshing waters of Sto. Niño Cold Spring and the powerful currents of Tuasan Falls provide essential relief from the tropical heat, acting as natural cooling stations for weary travelers. These wonders are interspersed with poignant remnants of the island's history, specifically the Old Church Ruins and the Sunken Cemetery. These sites stand as silent, solemn witnesses to the region's volcanic history—reminders of the eruptions that reshaped the land and the enduring resilience of the local culture.

Complementing this physical landscape is a culinary tradition that serves as a vital pillar of the local economy. Traditional offerings, such as Surol—a comforting native chicken soup infused with lemongrass and oregano—offer a profound taste of the island's agricultural heritage. The use of endemic ingredients is further showcased by kiping, a large, crispy cassava chip typically paired with a rich, sweet latik sauce. The evolution of the local food scene, marked by the emergence of diverse dining establishments like Txaleta de Camiguin, suggests a growing capacity to cater to a more sophisticated demographic of tourists by elevating traditional flavors through modern culinary techniques.

However, the journey to Camiguin is not without its hurdles. The logistical demands—often requiring a taxing sequence of flights, ferry crossings, and long road trips—can influence the duration and frequency of visitor stays. This accessibility gap underscores the importance of the local government’s commitment to sustainable development. By investing in the preservation of both natural resources and historical assets, the administration aims to harmonize the economic imperatives of tourism with the long-term environmental and cultural integrity of the community. Ensuring that Camiguin’s unique volcanic identity remains intact is not merely an environmental goal, but a necessity for the island's continued survival as a premier destination.
