The Algorithmic Divide: Why AI in Schools Must Not Leave the Working Class Behind

Photo provided by Baguio City Government / News Agency

Mayor Benjamin Magalong’s call for the responsible integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in schools marks a critical pivot in the Philippine educational landscape. As generative AI becomes a pervasive tool in global academia, the local government’s move to establish a framework for its use reflects a growing recognition of the technology's power to reshape human intellect, labor, and the very structure of learning. However, the conversation must move beyond the technicalities of 'how' to use these tools and address the systemic inequities they risk exacerbating.

For the average Filipino student, education remains the primary vehicle for social mobility. In a country where the gap between the elite and the working class is widening, the introduction of AI into the classroom introduces a new, invisible variable: the digital divide. If AI becomes the primary engine for academic success, those without consistent high-speed internet, stable electricity, or high-end hardware are effectively sidelined before they even enter the workforce. We risk creating a two-tier education system where the wealthy leap ahead into an AI-augmented future while the poor are left with under-resourced, traditional methods.

We must look beyond the glossy 'innovation' narrative often presented in municipal press releases. For the child of a daily commuter or a minimum-wage worker, 'responsible use' is a moot point if the basic infrastructure to access these tools is missing. The state’s responsibility is not merely to provide guidelines for teachers, but to ensure that the tools of the future are not exclusive privileges of the few. Without a concerted effort to democratize access to high-speed connectivity and hardware, AI will function as a gatekeeper rather than a gateway.

Furthermore, the rise of AI in education must be viewed through the lens of future employment. As automation threatens entry-level roles in the service and manufacturing sectors—the very sectors that absorb the bulk of the working-class labor force—the educational system must pivot. It must teach students to master AI as a tool while doubling down on human-centric skills that machines cannot replicate: critical thinking, complex problem-solving, and empathetic communication.\n

Ultimately, Magalong’s initiative serves as a necessary starting point, but it must be paired with a robust infrastructure mandate. True 'responsible use' requires a state-led effort to ensure that the digital frontier does not become a new frontier of inequality. Education must remain a bridge to opportunity, ensuring that the next generation of workers is equipped to lead in an automated world, regardless of the zip code they call home.
