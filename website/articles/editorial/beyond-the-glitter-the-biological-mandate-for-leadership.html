Beyond the Glitter: The Biological Mandate for Leadership

By Palanyag Editor • November 25, 2026

Interaksyon

We are a nation of spectators. We sit before glowing screens, hearts racing, waiting for the moment a crown is placed upon a head, convinced that for a few seconds, our collective pride is validated. We consume the spectacle of pageantry like a national ritual, often mistaking the shimmer of a sequined gown for the substance of our identity. This obsession with the aesthetic of success often serves as a convenient distraction from the grit of governance, allowing us to substitute vicarious triumph for actual civic engagement. But every so often, amidst the deafening cheers and the manufactured glamour of the Miss Universe stage, a truth slips through the cracks of the artifice.

When Bea Millan-Windorski stood in the Top 5 of the 75th Miss Universe competition, she did more than represent the Philippines; she offered a mirror to our collective psyche. Asked what the world needs most from its leaders, she did not reach for the usual platitudes of 'unity' or 'compassion'—words that have become hollowed out by decades of political misuse and empty campaign promises. Instead, she reached for the sea. Drawing from a decade of volunteering with sea turtles, she proposed a 'turtle mentality'—a biological imperative where the strongest hatchlings stay behind to ensure the weakest make it to the ocean.

It is a stinging indictment of our current state that such a simple, natural observation feels revolutionary. In the Philippines, we have long been haunted by the 'crab mentality'—that corrosive, reflexive impulse to pull down anyone who dares to climb higher. This is not merely a social quirk; it is a systemic failure that stifles progress. We see it in our political landscapes, where character assassination is preferred over substantive policy debate, and where the goal is to neutralize the opponent rather than solve the problem. We see it in our digital spaces, where the impulse to humiliate and 'cancel' often outweighs the desire to enlighten or engage. We have built a culture that celebrates the individual 'rise' while remaining chillingly indifferent to the drowning of the many.

Millan-Windorski’s advocacy for climate-displaced communities adds a layer of urgent, socio-political weight to her words. For an archipelago like ours, the climate crisis is not a theoretical debate for academics; it is an existential threat that arrives with every super typhoon and rising tide. When she speaks of leaders listening to nature, she is not calling for mysticism; she is demanding a fundamental realignment of power. A leader who adopts the turtle mentality would prioritize the resilience of the most vulnerable coastal village over the expansion of a billionaire’s luxury resort. They would recognize that a nation’s strength is not measured by its highest peak or its most glittering skyline, but by the safety and security of its lowest valley.

We must ask ourselves: Are we content with beauty that merely speaks well, or do we demand beauty that acts? A crown is a heavy thing, but the responsibility it represents should be heavier. If we can applaud a queen for suggesting that the strong should protect the weak, why do we find it so difficult to demand that same principle from the men and women sitting in our own halls of power? The applause must end, and the scrutiny must begin.
