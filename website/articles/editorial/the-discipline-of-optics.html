The Discipline of Optics

By Palanyag Editor • November 13, 2026

INQUIRER.net

The world is descending upon Metro Manila, bringing with it the heavy weight of geopolitical maneuvering and the sharp, unforgiving glare of international scrutiny. As the 49th ASEAN Summit approaches, the Philippines is not merely preparing a venue; it is preparing a performance. The capital is being transformed into a high-security stage where every movement is choreographed and every interaction is scrutinized by a global audience.

With over 19,000 police officers and thousands of other personnel deployed across the capital, the state is signaling a massive show of order. Brig. Gen. Christopher Abrahano of the NCRPO has issued a stern directive: uphold the highest standards of discipline. He has made it clear that the uniform is not a license to intimidate, harass, or take advantage of the public. On the surface, this is a commendable reminder that the Philippine National Police (PNP) must remain a servant of the people, even when the pressure of high-stakes diplomacy is at its peak. Such directives are necessary to maintain public trust and prevent the chaos that often accompanies large-scale security operations.

But we must look past the polished statements and the tactical deployments. Why is this directive being issued with such pointed urgency now? It is because, during an international summit, the eyes of the world are not just on the leaders discussing trade and regional security; they are on the streets. The conduct of our security forces becomes a metric by which our democracy is judged. In an era of instant communication, a single instance of misconduct can be broadcast globally in seconds, undermining the very stability the state seeks to project. When the presence of powerful figures like US President Donald Trump is expected, the stakes for 'optics' become astronomical. The world is watching to see if the Philippines can manage its own house with dignity and respect for human rights.

There is a profound tension between the state’s desire for a seamless, orderly event and the fundamental right of its citizens to dissent. The PNP promises 'maximum tolerance' toward protesters, yet it simultaneously warns that any assembly conducted outside of designated 'freedom parks' will be met with legal action and arrests. This is the inherent paradox of the modern democratic state: we permit the right to protest, but only within the narrow, sanitized corridors we have deemed convenient for the spectacle of governance. By confining dissent to specific, controlled zones, the state effectively attempts to mute the message while maintaining the appearance of liberty.

We must ask the uncomfortable question: Is the discipline of our police a genuine reflection of institutional reform, or is it a temporary costume donned for the sake of global prestige? If the restraint of our officers is fueled primarily by the fear of international embarrassment, then it is not discipline—it is theater. True authority does not require a global audience to behave with decency. It does not need the watchful eyes of foreign diplomats to respect the rights of its own citizens. Real reform is measured by how a state treats its people when no one is watching, not by how well it performs when the spotlight is brightest.

As the summit unfolds, let us watch the streets as closely as the delegates watch the podiums. Let us see if the 'highest standards' hold when the cameras turn away, or if the mask of discipline slips the moment the international spotlight fades.
