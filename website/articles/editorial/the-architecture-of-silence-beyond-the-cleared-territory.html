The Architecture of Silence: Beyond the 'Cleared' Territory

By Palanyag Editor • November 14, 2026

philstar.com

Peace is often measured by the absence of gunfire. In the sprawling landscapes of Regions 11 and 12, the recent declaration that various towns have been 'cleared' of the New People’s Army (NPA) is being heralded as a triumph of statecraft. Through a cocktail of non-hostile backchannel initiatives and intensified intelligence cooperation between the 10th Infantry Division and regional police offices, the government is signaling that the era of insurgency in these hinterlands is waning.

But for the common Filipino, a 'cleared' area is not a synonym for a 'prosperous' area.

The recent intelligence workshop in Davao City, which brought together the Army, the Police, the Coast Guard, and the National Intelligence Agency, underscores a tightening of the state’s grip. This expanded cooperation is not merely about hunting rebels; it has pivoted toward dismantling the shadow economies that thrive in the periphery—illegal mining operations and offshore gambling networks that have seen dozens of foreign and local operators hauled to justice. This is a necessary pivot. When the state fails to regulate its resources and its borders, it creates a vacuum that both insurgents and criminals are all too eager to fill.

However, we must look closer at the mechanics of this peace. The reports highlight the 'multi-sectoral' approach and the support of local executives like Governors Reynaldo Tamayo, Jr. and Emmylou Taliño-Mendoza. This synergy between the uniformed services and local government units (LGUs) is the backbone of stability. Without the cooperation of the mayors and governors, intelligence is just noise, and military operations are just expensive skirmishes.

Yet, the critical question remains: what happens when the intelligence officers leave the room and the patrol cars return to the station?

Security is a prerequisite for development, but it is not development itself. You can clear a mountain of insurgents, but if the mountain remains stripped of its copper and gold by illegal miners, or if the local community remains starved of basic services, the 'peace' achieved is nothing more than a temporary truce. The socio-economic improvements mentioned by officials must be more than a post-conflict talking point; they must be the primary objective.

We must challenge the narrative that peace is a destination reached once the last rebel surrenders. True peace is the presence of justice, the rule of law, and the tangible arrival of opportunity in the farthest reaches of the provinces. If the intensified intelligence cooperation in Mindanao only results in better arrests but not better lives, then we have merely perfected the art of policing a graveyard of missed opportunities. The state must ensure that the silence following the gunfire is not the silence of poverty, but the quiet hum of a functioning, thriving society.
