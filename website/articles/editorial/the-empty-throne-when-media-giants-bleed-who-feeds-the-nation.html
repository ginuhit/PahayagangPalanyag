The Empty Throne: When Media Giants Bleed, Who Feeds the Nation?

By Palanyag Editor • November 14, 2026

RAPPLER

The giants are bleeding.

For decades, the landscape of the Filipino consciousness was carved out by a handful of titans. We looked to the television screen to see our leaders, to find our stories, and to understand our world. But the recent financial disclosures from the country’s broadcast heavyweights suggest that the throne is cracking. GMA Network, the undisputed leader in television ratings, reported a staggering 98% plunge in net income for the third quarter of 2026. From a robust P2 billion to a mere P32.85 million—a fall so steep it feels less like a business downturn and more like a structural collapse.

To the casual observer, these numbers are just corporate math. But to the critical citizen, they are a siren song of a changing reality. GMA’s decline is a post-election hangover, a vacuum left by the absence of the massive political advertising spend that characterized the 2025 mid-terms. Yet, the deeper rot is more systemic. The industry is witnessing a Great Migration: the mass exodus of both eyeballs and advertising pesos from the traditional airwaves to the digital abyss. The very medium that once unified the archipelago is being cannibalized by the algorithms of social media.

Then there is the case of ABS-CBN. While GMA fights to keep its margins from evaporating, the Lopez-led entity remains deep in the red, grappling with a multi-billion peso loss. But here, the story takes a pivot. Deprived of its broadcast license, ABS-CBN has stopped trying to be a broadcaster and has started trying to be a cultural exporter. Through the global reach of BINI and the star power of the DonBelle phenomenon, they are finding a way to survive not through the airwaves, but through the global appetite for Filipino talent. One is fighting to hold onto the old world; the other is frantically building a new one.

This leaves us with a haunting question: In this transition, what happens to the Filipino public?

As these media empires struggle to balance their books, they are forced to make choices. When profit margins shrink, does the quality of investigative journalism suffer? When the race for digital revenue becomes a battle for 'clicks,' does the nuance of truth get sacrificed for the dopamine hit of viral entertainment? We are moving from an era of mass information to an era of fragmented influence.

We must ask ourselves if we are being served a diet of substance or merely a steady stream of distraction. As the giants bleed, the void they leave behind is not being filled by more truth, but by more noise. The question is no longer just about how these companies survive—it is about whether we, the audience, can survive the chaos of their transformation.
