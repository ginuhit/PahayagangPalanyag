The Weight of Giants: Diplomacy on a Razor's Edge

By Palanyag Editor • November 14, 2026

INQUIRER.net

The shadows cast by giants are never small. When Donald Trump lands on Philippine soil this November, the air in Manila will grow heavy with more than just the humidity of the monsoon season. It will be heavy with the weight of global geopolitics, the friction of competing empires, and the precariousness of our own national sovereignty.

Ambassador Jose Manuel “Babe” Romualdez has confirmed that the former American leader’s arrival has been pushed to November 18–19. While a delay in scheduling might seem like a mere bureaucratic hiccup, the timing is anything but incidental. This visit coincides with the 49th ASEAN Summit, a gathering where the Philippines sits at the helm of Southeast Asian discourse. As President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. prepares to host regional leaders at the PICC, the real conversation won't just be about economic cooperation or the humanitarian crisis in Myanmar. The real conversation will be written in the salt and spray of the West Philippine Sea.

For decades, the 1951 Mutual Defense Treaty has been the bedrock of our relationship with the United States. It is our shield, our insurance policy, and, to some, our greatest vulnerability. As tensions with Beijing continue to simmer and boil over regarding maritime claims, the presence of a figure as unpredictable and transformative as Trump brings a complex duality to our shores. To some, he represents the decisive muscle needed to deter Chinese encroachment through a policy of strength. To others, his brand of 'America First' diplomacy represents a volatile wildcard—a transactional approach that could prioritize American interests at the expense of regional stability, potentially dragging the Philippines into a conflict we are ill-equipped to fight.

We must look past the red carpets and the formal handshakes. We must look at the strategic calculus being performed in the closed rooms of Pasay City. Are we leveraging this visit to solidify our stance on maritime security, or are we simply providing a convenient stage for a superpower's power play? When the leaders of the world meet, the common Filipino is often relegated to the role of a spectator—watching from the sidelines as the map of our nation is redrawn by hands that do not hold our plow or our nets.

Sovereignty is not a gift granted by allies; it is a condition maintained by the vigilance of a people. As the world's eyes turn toward Manila, we must demand more than just security guarantees. We must demand a foreign policy that is not merely reactive to the whims of Washington or the aggression of Beijing, but one that is unapologetically centered on the Filipino interest. The giants are coming, but we must ensure that we are not merely the ground they walk upon.
