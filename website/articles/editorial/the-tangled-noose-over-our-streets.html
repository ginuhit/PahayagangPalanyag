The Tangled Noose Over Our Streets

By Palanyag Editor • November 13, 2026

Daily Tribune

A Grade 3 pupil reaches out—perhaps to catch a falling leaf or to steady himself on a walk home—and in a flash of blinding light and agonizing heat, his future is severed. He survives, but not with both hands. In Cavite, a twelve-year-old boy didn't even get the chance to heal; he simply died. These are not mere statistical outliers or isolated tragedies born of bad luck. They are the predictable, violent outcomes of a systemic failure draped across our urban skyline.

We call them “spaghetti wires”—a term that sounds almost whimsical, almost harmless. But there is nothing playful about a tangled, chaotic mass of live electrical lines, fiber optics, and heavy communication cables hanging precariously over the heads of pedestrians, commuters, and children. This is not a culinary metaphor; it is a death trap. The term itself serves as a linguistic veil, softening the reality of the lethal clutter that defines our streets.

The anatomy of this crisis is a masterclass in bureaucratic finger-pointing. When wires spark or a pole buckles under the sheer, suffocating weight of unmanaged cables, the blame game begins with practiced ease. Power distributors like Meralco point to internet service providers (ISPs) who allegedly attach lines to electric posts without proper authorization or structural assessment. The ISPs, in turn, operate in a regulatory gray zone, driven by the frantic pace of rapid digital expansion and minimal oversight. Meanwhile, the public is left to navigate a labyrinth of copper and plastic that grows thicker and more lethal with every new subscription signed. This lack of a centralized, unified management system for utility poles allows negligence to flourish in the gaps between agencies.

Senator Raffy Tulfo’s call for an investigation is long overdue, but an investigation is merely a post-mortem. The real tragedy lies in the fact that we already possess the tools to prevent these deaths, yet we refuse to wield them. We have local ordinances. We have various bills in the 19th Congress specifically aimed at the bundling and removal of non-operational or "dead" wires. Yet, these legislative efforts languish in the dark, gathering dust in committees, much like the abandoned, dead wires hanging over our streets.

This is the grim reality of the modern Philippines: we are obsessed with connectivity but indifferent to safety. We demand high-speed internet and constant power, yet we are willing to tolerate a chaotic, unregulated infrastructure that treats human life as an acceptable casualty of progress. When laws exist on paper but fail on the pavement, the law itself becomes as tangled and useless as the wires it seeks to regulate.

We must stop treating these incidents as "accidents." An accident is an unpredictable event. These deaths are the result of systemic negligence, a lack of enforcement, and a refusal to hold utility companies accountable for the physical integrity of our shared public spaces. If the weight of these wires is enough to make a utility pole collapse, then our governance is clearly buckling under the same pressure. Until we demand more than just "investigations"—until we demand the immediate, mandatory, and strictly enforced cleaning of our skies—we are simply waiting for the next child to reach for a dream and touch a live wire instead.
