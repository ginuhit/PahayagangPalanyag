The Anatomy of a Windfall

By Palanyag Editor • November 13, 2026

INQUIRER.net

Twenty-five billion, eight hundred twenty million pesos.

To the average Filipino, a number of this magnitude is an abstraction, a mathematical ghost that haunts the periphery of daily survival. We talk in terms of daily minimum wages, the fluctuating price of a kilo of rice, or the rising cost of a jeepney fare. But for the LT Group, P25.82 billion is not a ghost; it is a concrete, record-breaking reality. In the first nine months of 2026, this conglomerate didn't just grow—it surged, posting a 14 percent increase in net income that feels almost disconnected from the economic anxieties of the masses.

To understand this windfall, one must look at the pillars upon which it stands. The largest contributor was the Philippine National Bank (PNB), accounting for P11.95 billion. The report notes that net interest income climbed as the loan portfolio expanded. Here lies the first tension: in an economy where credit is often the only lifeline for small businesses and struggling households, the expansion of a bank’s profit is inextricably linked to the cost of debt. When interest income rises, it is because the cost of borrowing has become a more significant engine for capital accumulation.

Then, there is the smoke. The tobacco segment, led by PMFTC, contributed P9.35 billion. What is most striking is not just the profit, but the resilience of the volume. Despite annual excise tax increases and higher cigarette prices, the offtake volume rose to 16.4 billion sticks. This is a grim indicator of the socio-economic landscape. When prices rise, the most basic human vices often do not diminish; they merely become more expensive burdens for the poor. The 'sin tax' is intended to curb consumption and fund healthcare, yet the corporate ledger shows that these tax hikes are effectively absorbed by a market that continues to consume, driven by a cycle of dependency that remains untouched by policy.

Add to this the contributions from Tanduay Distillers and the various other holdings, and you see a portfolio built on the fundamental coping mechanisms of a stressed society: debt, tobacco, and alcohol.

We are told that these numbers represent a healthy economy. But we must ask: healthy for whom? A record profit is a triumph of management and market dominance, certainly. But when the engines of growth are fueled by the interest on loans and the sale of addictive commodities, we must interrogate the quality of that growth. Is our economy truly advancing if its most significant victories are extracted from the very struggles and dependencies of its people?

Wealth is being concentrated at the summit, while the base of the pyramid continues to pay the premium for survival. The question is no longer whether the giants can grow, but whether their growth requires the continued exhaustion of the many.
