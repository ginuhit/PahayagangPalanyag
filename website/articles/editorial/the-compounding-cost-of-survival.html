The Compounding Cost of Survival

By Palanyag Editor • November 13, 2026

INQUIRER.net

The math of survival is becoming increasingly difficult to balance. For the average Filipino, inflation is not a dry statistic presented in a government briefing; it is the visible shrinking of a grocery bag, the hesitation before tapping a transport card, and the quiet anxiety of a household trying to stretch a paycheck that seems to evaporate the moment it hits the palm of a hand. It is the silent erosion of dignity, where the basic necessities of life are being traded for the mere ability to endure another day.

In the highlands of the Cordillera, these numbers are no longer just concerning—they are alarming. With inflation hitting 9.3 percent in Baguio City and 9.2 percent in the broader region, the mountain resort city is facing a peak that signals a deepening economic crisis. We are witnessing a 'compounding effect,' a systemic domino effect of economic pressures where one failure feeds into the next. A weakened peso makes imported fuel more expensive, which in turn escalates the cost of transporting Benguet vegetables to the lowlands. Because of the region's unique geography, logistics are the lifeblood of the economy; when the cost of moving goods rises, the price of the plate rises. When the price of the plate rises, the purchasing power of the worker collapses, creating a cycle of poverty that is difficult to break.

The data reveals a brutal reality. Rice, a staple that defines our national identity and survival, saw an inflation rate of 20.2 percent in October alone. This is not merely a fluctuation in market price; it is a direct threat to food security. When the most fundamental source of sustenance becomes a luxury, the social contract begins to fray. Coupled with a staggering 48.4 percent inflation in gasoline, the 'second round of risk' mentioned by statisticians is no longer a theoretical warning—it is a lived experience. Every time a jeepney fare ticks up by a few pesos, it acts as a regressive tax on the poorest of the poor, a surcharge on the act of simply going to work. For the laborer, these incremental increases are not minor adjustments; they are the difference between a balanced meal and an empty stomach.

What is most striking is the geopolitical irony of our local struggle. The dinner tables of the Cordillera are being dictated by the Strait of Hormuz and the protracted wars in the Middle East. We are tethered to a global supply chain that leaves our domestic economy vulnerable to tremors thousands of miles away. While we cannot control the movements of foreign powers or the fluctuations of global oil markets, we must critically examine our domestic resilience. Are we doing enough to insulate our most vulnerable citizens from these global shocks? A nation’s strength should be measured by its ability to shield its people from external volatility, yet we remain exposed.

There is a haunting paradox in the fact that Baguio remains a tourist favorite despite these skyrocketing costs. It suggests a bifurcated economy: a thriving 'experience economy' for the visitor, and a struggling 'survival economy' for the resident. We often praise the Filipino spirit of resilience, but we must ask ourselves: at what point does resilience become a convenient excuse for systemic neglect? We cannot simply 'endure' a cost of living that outpaces our ability to earn. It is time to move beyond the rhetoric of perseverance and demand structural safeguards—subsidies, logistical reforms, and wage adjustments—that protect the Filipino household from the compounding weight of a volatile world.
