The Ghost in the Machine: Who Really Holds the Remote?

By Palanyag Editor • November 25, 2026

Rappler

The ghost is in the machine, and it is currently being negotiated in Barbados.

At the Bridgetown Special Summit on AI Safety, UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres and Barbados Prime Minister Mia Amor Mottley sat across from the architects of our digital future—tech giants, scientists, and government officials. Their mission sounds noble: to ensure that artificial intelligence is developed safely and that its benefits are shared widely across the globe. It is a plea for international cooperation in an era where code moves faster than law, attempting to build regulatory guardrails around a technology that is already outrunning our ability to govern it.

But for the average Filipino, 'AI safety' sounds like a luxury problem, a conversation meant for the air-conditioned halls of the Global North. While Western regulators debate the nuances of 'algorithmic bias' or the long-term 'existential risk' of superintelligence, our immediate reality is far more visceral. The Philippines is a global epicenter for social media consumption, which inadvertently makes us the perfect, high-frequency laboratory for AI-driven disinformation. When an algorithm decides which piece of fabricated news reaches your feed to sway a local election or incite social unrest, that is not an abstract 'safety' issue; it is a direct, surgical assault on our democracy.

Then there is the matter of the bread on our tables. The Philippines is a nation built on the backs of the BPO industry and the service sectors—industries that are currently staring down the barrel of rapid, aggressive automation. If the 'benefits' of AI are not truly democratized, as the Bridgetown summit hopes, we are looking at a future of extreme economic divergence. We risk a world where the immense wealth generated by artificial intelligence is concentrated in the hands of a few firms in Silicon Valley, while the labor displaced by that same intelligence is dumped into the streets of Manila and Cebu, creating a new class of the technologically unemployed.

The digital divide has evolved. It is no longer just about who has a smartphone and who does not; it has become a divide between those who own the intelligence and those who are merely processed by it. This is a new form of data colonialism, where the Global South provides the raw material—our clicks, our faces, and our voices—to train models that we will eventually be forced to rent back from the North. If we do not demand a seat at the table where these rules are written, we will remain nothing more than a massive, unpaid dataset used to train the very machines that may eventually render our skills obsolete.

International cooperation is a necessary start, but it is not a solution. We must move beyond mere 'safety' discussions and demand 'equity.' We must ask the hard questions: Who owns the data? Who controls the logic? And most importantly, who is held accountable when the machine errs?

As we watch the leaders in Barbados shake hands, we must look closer at our own screens. In a world where the truth can be manufactured by a single prompt, the most radical act of survival is to think for ourselves.
