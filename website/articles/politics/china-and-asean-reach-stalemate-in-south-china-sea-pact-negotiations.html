China and ASEAN Reach Stalemate in South China Sea Pact Negotiations

By the News Desk•November 11, 2026

INQUIRER.net

Diplomatic efforts between China and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) have reached a critical juncture as negotiations for a maritime pact in the South China Sea continue to face delays. While both parties have acknowledged significant progress in establishing a framework for maritime cooperation, officials have confirmed that a finalized agreement is unlikely to be signed before the conclusion of the current calendar year. The negotiations aim to create a Code of Conduct (CoC) designed to manage tensions, prevent accidental military escalations, and regulate activities within the contested waters.

The discussions have centered on several high-stakes pillars, including the protection of marine environments, the management of fishing zones, and the preservation of freedom of navigation. However, the path to a consensus remains obstructed by fundamental disagreements regarding the legal weight of the pact. While China has historically pushed for a non-binding framework that accommodates its expansive claims, several ASEAN member states, including the Philippines, have consistently advocated for a legally binding agreement that strictly adheres to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). These differing priorities on sovereignty and enforcement mechanisms remain the primary hurdles to a swift resolution.

For the Philippines, the absence of a finalized pact this year underscores the persistent risks faced by Filipino fisherfolk and maritime assets in the West Philippine Sea. The continued lack of a definitive treaty means that the current status quo—marked by frequent standoffs and the presence of Chinese maritime militia—will persist into the next year. The Philippine government remains committed to a dual-track strategy: pursuing diplomatic dialogue through ASEAN while simultaneously upholding the 2016 Arbitral Award, which invalidated China's claims over the majority of the contested area. The slow pace of these negotiations highlights the complexity of balancing regional economic ties with national security imperatives.\n

Looking ahead, the focus will shift toward internal ASEAN consultations to harmonize the positions of member states, whose stances vary based on their individual security and trade relationships with Beijing. While the progress reported by officials provides a glimmer of hope for long-term regional stability, the transition from a mere 'code of conduct' to a comprehensive, enforceable treaty remains the ultimate diplomatic challenge. The international community will closely monitor the resumption of these talks in the coming months to see if a compromise can be reached to mitigate the growing risks of maritime friction.
