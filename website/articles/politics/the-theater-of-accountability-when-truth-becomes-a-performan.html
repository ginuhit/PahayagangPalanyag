The Theater of Accountability: When Truth Becomes a Performance

P | Politics

The impeachment process is intended to be the ultimate check and balance, a somber mechanism designed to purge the highest offices of incompetence or corruption. When Senator Panfilo Lacson characterizes witness testimonies as a 'mockery,' he is not merely critiquing a lack of decorum; he is sounding an alarm on the erosion of our constitutional safeguards. A trial that descends into farce loses its ability to serve as a deterrent, transforming a grave judicial proceeding into a spectator sport where the only winners are those who master the art of the performative.

This trivialization of testimony suggests a systemic malaise where the 'diskarte' of political survival takes precedence over the pursuit of objective truth. In a landscape where every statement is calibrated for social media clips and partisan headlines, the nuance of fact-finding is often sacrificed for the immediacy of the 'gotcha' moment. When witnesses provide testimonies that are intentionally vague, contradictory, or overtly theatrical, they undermine the very purpose of the inquiry. They treat the microphone not as a tool for revelation, but as a stage for personal branding, prioritizing the optics of the moment over the substance of the record.

The danger of this 'mockery' lies in its corrosive effect on public trust. For the average Filipino, who navigates a reality of rising costs and systemic inefficiencies, the spectacle of a hollowed-out impeachment process reinforces a pervasive cynicism. If the mechanisms of accountability are perceived as a comedy of errors, the public's faith in the rule of law is inevitably diminished. It suggests that the 'trapo' culture—where politics is a game of optics—has successfully infiltrated the most sacred of our democratic institutions, turning a search for justice into a rehearsal for the next election cycle.

While the threat of contempt is a necessary legal recourse, it serves only as a reactive measure to a proactive cultural problem. We must move beyond the mere policing of witness behavior and address the underlying incentive structures that encourage such behavior. Why is it more advantageous for a witness to provide a 'mockery' than a meaningful truth? It is because our political ecosystem often rewards the loudest, most sensationalist performance rather than the quiet, rigorous integrity of a factual account. When the truth is inconvenient to the narrative, the narrative is allowed to supersede the fact.

Ultimately, the sanctity of the impeachment trial must be defended against the encroachment of political theater. A democracy is only as strong as its ability to hold its leaders to account through honest, substantive, and rigorous processes. When we allow the trial to become a farce, we are not just mocking the witnesses; we are mocking the very aspirations of a nation seeking a government that functions with integrity rather than just a facade of activity. The preservation of truth in our halls of power is not a mere procedural detail; it is a prerequisite for a functioning republic.
